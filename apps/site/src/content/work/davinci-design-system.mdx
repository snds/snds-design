The LinkedIn homepage team needed a systematic way to guide the many redesigns
coming down the pipe. What began as "use Company Pages as a model" became
something larger: a centralized, documented system — with code — that let teams
cut the complexity and time of a feature redesign. At the time, a coded design
system with a shared specification language was close to unprecedented.

## The problem

Before DaVinci, scoping a redesign took **6–12 weeks**. Designers and engineers
spoke different languages about the same components, and every team rebuilt the
same patterns from scratch.

> **The challenge:** give product teams an efficient way to redesign their
> products on top of the work the homepage team had already done.

## Approach

**A shared nomenclature.** We interviewed across the org to close the
designer–engineer communication gap, then pared the language down to a schema of
human-legible verbs and terms that were still composable from an engineering
standpoint — one vocabulary both disciplines could use.

**Distribution.** We shipped UI kits for Photoshop, Illustrator, and Fireworks,
built an in-house documentation site, and ran office-hours review sessions to
validate and discover new patterns.

## Outcome

DaVinci cut technical debt by ~40%, sped implementation by ~35%, and shrank
timeline estimates by ~30%. Support tickets dropped 32% and on-site calls 46% —
and the remaining tickets shifted from "how does this work?" to feature requests.
CEO Jeff Weiner championed the system company-wide, and it spawned a successor,
Art Deco, that informed LinkedIn's visual direction for years. The throughline:
less friction, more growth.
